# Structural Patterns

Named chapter architectures for nonfiction/business books, especially
suited to technology and AI topics. Every Block 1 option must be
grounded in one of these — modified to fit the chapter's specific
argument, but not invented from nothing.

---

## Pattern A — Problem → Ceiling → Reframe → New Model

The reader's current approach works, until it doesn't. Show where it
breaks down, why the obvious fixes don't hold, then introduce a genuinely
different mental model.

**Shape:** Familiar approach (works, for now) → Where it hits a wall →
Why the obvious fix doesn't work either → The reframe → What the new
model makes possible → What this demands of the reader now.

**Best for:** chapters arguing an existing paradigm has run out of
road — classic "personalization has hit a ceiling, here's presence
instead" structure.

---

## Pattern B — Before/After Case, Then Principle

Open on a concrete, vivid before/after (a product, a team, a company)
that embodies the chapter's thesis. Extract the underlying principle
from that case, then show the principle generalizes.

**Shape:** Cold open on a specific transformation → What changed and why
it worked → Naming the underlying principle → Evidence the principle
generalizes beyond this one case → What breaks if you ignore the
principle → Reader's move.

**Best for:** chapters with one especially strong case study or
flagship example available from research.

---

## Pattern C — Myth → Autopsy → Replacement

Open by stating a widely-believed claim in the field. Take it apart
methodically. Replace it with what's actually true, backed by evidence.

**Shape:** State the myth as most people believe it → Why it was
believable / where it came from → The autopsy (specific evidence it
doesn't hold) → What's actually true instead → Why the truth is
uncomfortable or counterintuitive → What changes once you accept it.

**Best for:** contrarian chapters, chapters correcting a popular
misconception in AI/tech discourse.

---

## Pattern D — Escalating Stakes (Three Rounds)

Structure the chapter as three escalating rounds of the same underlying
tension, each round raising the stakes and sophistication of the
argument.

**Shape:** Round 1 — simple version of the problem → Round 2 — the
problem gets harder/more nuanced → Round 3 — the hardest version, where
the real insight lives → Synthesis across all three rounds → Reader's
move.

**Best for:** technical chapters where a concept genuinely gets more
sophisticated as you dig in (e.g. agent autonomy: simple automation →
semi-autonomous → fully agentic, each with different trust
implications).

---

## Pattern E — Tour of the Landscape, Then Synthesis

Walk the reader through several distinct approaches/products/philosophies
in the space, treating each fairly, then synthesize what they collectively
reveal.

**Shape:** Why a tour is useful here → Stop 1 (approach/company A) →
Stop 2 (approach/company B) → Stop 3 (approach/company C) → What the
tour reveals when you step back → The synthesis / the chapter's actual
argument → Reader's move.

**Best for:** chapters comparing competing philosophies (e.g. different
companies' approaches to AI safety, or different agent-architecture
philosophies) where no single case is dominant enough to anchor the
whole chapter.

---

## Pattern F — Personal Stakes → Systemic Pattern

Open close and personal (a scenario the reader can picture themselves
in), then zoom out to show this is a systemic pattern, not an isolated
incident.

**Shape:** A specific, relatable scenario → The reader recognizes
themselves in it → Zoom out: this happens systemically, here's the
scale → Why it happens (the underlying mechanism) → What changes the
pattern → Reader's move.

**Best for:** chapters about how AI tools change day-to-day work or
decision-making at an individual level, before making a broader
industry claim.

---

## Choosing and modifying a pattern

- Pick the pattern whose *shape* best matches what the actual research
  supports — don't force material into a pattern it doesn't fit.
- Modifications are expected and encouraged: swap which case anchors
  Pattern B, add a fourth round to Pattern D if the material genuinely
  needs it, merge two "stops" in Pattern E if they turn out too similar.
- Two options can legitimately use the same base pattern if their
  modifications are substantively different — different case, different
  emotional arc, or different technology-section placement.
